**Year 1025.** Mirchfor was cut off from the water in 1025, and the wealth the trade had brought to Mirchfor was gone, and the town had no say in any of it. Nothing at Mirchfor was destroyed and everything at Mirchfor stopped.

**Year 1050.** The water rose at Mirchfor in 1050 and took a fortnight to leave, and the grain in store at Mirchfor was lost, and the grain was the year. Nobody at Mirchfor was compensated, because nobody at Mirchfor had insured anything: the clerks filed it under the Mirchfor Flood and moved on.

**Year 1075.** Nothing was disputed when the First Magnate died, and Thorvorluk took the chair. That is rare enough in this record to be worth the ink, and Thorvorluk held the chair for the rest of the reign. The First Magnate changes and the arrangement does not, and Thorvorluk inherits it entire.

**Year 1075.** The wind held onto the Quillton shore and would not let go of it. And what was insured at Quillton was replaced and what was not was not. The quay at Quillton held because the quay had money spent on it. And it is entered as the Great Storm of 1075 and dated to the equinox.

**Year 1075.** Foldenbriar turned up something the surveys had missed. The money arrived before the assessors did, which is the only order that ever helps Foldenbriar. Nothing about Foldenbriar had changed except what was known about it. And the clerks called it the Foldenbriar Find without saying what it was.

**Year 1100.** Harroor succeeded the old Sovereign and nobody contested it, so the ledgers at Rakemergate did not move, which is the only test of a succession that matters. Harroor inherits a Sovereign's ledger and none of a Sovereign's choices.

**Year 1100.** Deliel came to the censer by the ordinary route. And the ledgers at Rakemergate carried on without a correction. Across 20 regions the only thing that changed was a name, and the name is Deliel.

**Year 1100.** The Mirchfor banks gave and the river took the difference. And the river had done it before and the survey at Mirchfor said so. The count of the dead at Mirchfor is an estimate and always was; it is filed as the Mirchfor Flood, with the count left blank.

**Year 1100.** Sickness came to Durnton in 1100. The road out of Durnton was the only remedy anyone could afford. The blight at Durnton was decades old and nobody had been charged for it, so it stands as the Blacklung of 1100 in every copy that survives.

**Year 1100.** The sanctioned ground by Thulgruk Fell woke, and the Temple sealed the site at Thulgruk Fell and wrote the account. Nothing grows in the ring around Thulgruk Fell and nothing is expected to.

**Year 1100.** Thulgruk Fell rose, the constabulary line broke, and the mob held, so what Thulgruk Fell makes stays at Thulgruk Fell. Thulgruk Fell was free and hungry in the same year, and the record does not resolve that. The date survives as the Thulgruk Fell Rising, unofficially.

**Year 1125.** The lode under Rakemergate was mined to nothing, and the aetherworks stopped with it, so the town the yards had grown around emptied behind them, and the survey still lists Rakemergate at its old size. The magnates who left Rakemergate took the machinery and left the housing.

**Year 1125.** Somebody raised a tower on the dark ground at Thulgruk Fell; it was reported eventually, and by then everyone at Thulgruk Fell had used it. The charter forbidding it does not reach as far as Thulgruk Fell.

**Year 1125.** The wells at Umbere were reading low by midsummer and dry by the next. And the country the water had barely reached went to dust first, as it always does around Umbere. Nothing at Umbere was rationed, which is a decision even when nobody makes it; the Drought of 1125 is the entry, and it is a short one.

**Year 1125.** Quillton was under weather for three days and under water for one, and the crews at Quillton were not insured and the hulls were. What Quillton lost was a season, and a season is what it lives on. And the year survives as the Great Storm of 1125.

**Year 1125.** What had gone quiet at Deep Drokthak was loud again within a season; the innkeepers at Deep Drokthak did better out of it than the priests, and said so quietly. The Temple took its share at Deep Drokthak and the share was not small: the Rise of Deep Drokthak is the year in the Temple's own count.

**Year 1125.** The fever found Thulgruk Fell on ground already poisoned; the burial rolls at Thulgruk Fell ran longer than the tax rolls that year. The ground around Thulgruk Fell was fouled by a decision, and the fever was not: the Long Cough of 1125 is what the roads out of Thulgruk Fell still call that year.

**Year 1150.** The Corvine League began taking an interest in Mirchfor Harbor, and nothing was agreed at Mirchfor Harbor, which is how these things start. Rakemergate saw it and chose to see nothing.

**Year 1150.** There was a body in 1150 and no agreed successor to it; the quarrel was settled the way quarrels over the chair are settled, and Zolgrumketh settled it. Nothing about the arrangement over 20 regions turned on whether the name was Zolgrumketh.

**Year 1150.** Browmerwell had no rain worth the name for two years. The poorest ground at Browmerwell was the first to be given up. The water that was left at Browmerwell went where water always goes, which is to whoever owns the channel, and the year went into the register as the Drought of 1150.

**Year 1150.** A find at Foldenbriar in 1150 changed what the ground was worth: the assessment at Foldenbriar caught up within three years. For a generation Foldenbriar was somewhere to arrive at; the register calls it the Foldenbriar Find.

**Year 1150.** Rakemergate hardened rather than yielded; the spoil trains were licensed to do openly what they had done anyway, and Rakemergate wrote it down. What Rakemergate met with force it had met with nothing for a decade.

**Year 1175.** Aetherworks were put up at Quarburg because the assay said so. And the copper reached Quarburg on the strength of an assay. The assessment at Quarburg was raised in the same season as the wages.

**Year 1175.** Mirchfor was under water from the wharves to the second street; the survey had marked it and the leases at Mirchfor were signed anyway. What stood high at Mirchfor stood dry, and what stood high was not where most people lived; the Mirchfor Flood is the heading and the count is left open.

**Year 1200.** The Hierarch died in 1200 and the succession went as written, to Liane, and the 20 regions carried on as though nothing had happened, because nothing had. What a Hierarch inherits is a ledger, and the ledger at Rakemergate did not change hands with Liane.

**Year 1200.** The water came at Hazeldton out of a clear autumn, and the shore that pays for the harbour in the good years paid again at Hazeldton. The fleet at Hazeldton was insured and the crews were not. And the clerks wrote the Great Storm of 1200 and the survivors agreed with them.

**Year 1225.** The succession of 1225 was uneventful, and Harnhold is the name it produced, so nobody in the 20 regions was consulted, and nobody expected to be. The 20 regions were told afterwards that Harnhold held it.

**Year 1225.** The streams that feed Peldby ran to gravel, and there was enough at Peldby for some, which is how a drought becomes a quarrel. Nothing at Peldby was rationed, which is a decision even unmade, so the Drought of 1225 is the name and the whole of the explanation.

**Year 1225.** The fold beneath High Murzhar released what it had been holding: the road out of High Murzhar was impassable in both directions. The pass above High Murzhar was the cheap route, and it was days before it was a route again. And the High Murzhar Quake is the entry, and it does not mention the ground.

**Year 1225.** A storm came off the water and stood over the Vorngard coast for three days, and the wharves at Vorngard were rebuilt within the year, and the houses behind them were not. The exposure that makes a harbour is the exposure that empties it, at Vorngard as everywhere, so the Great Storm of 1225 is the name that stuck.

**Year 1225.** What had been quiet at Deep Drokthak was loud again inside a season; the assessment at Deep Drokthak rose with the traffic, as it does. The coin came on foot, one pilgrim at a time, and Deep Drokthak counted every step of it, and the Rise of Deep Drokthak is what the faithful call it, and they call it that still.

**Year 1250.** The First Magnate died in 1250, and the transfer of the chair took a fortnight; what changed at Rakemergate was a name at the head of the page. What Ostrek inherits across 20 regions is a ledger, and it did not move.

**Year 1250.** High Murzhar sits on the fold, and in 1250 the fold moved: the survey had marked the ground under High Murzhar and the building went on regardless. The rebuilding at High Murzhar used the same ground and the same mortar, so it is filed as the High Murzhar Quake, under weather, which is wrong.

### The State of the Realm, Year 1250

By the last epoch, of 8 settled regions, 7 neither rose nor fell and 1 collapsed outright, and a deadhold keeps its name because striking it costs a clerk more than leaving it, and 12 of them were left.

12 holdings stand where a town was and is not.

1 place has come back under names nobody there had used before, though it stands under a name in a different tongue than the one the Rakemergate charts carried before.

Marton rose further than any other place in the record, so nothing Marton decided accounts for as much as where Marton was put.

At the close it is 14 to the Crown, 4 to the Temple, 0 to the magnates, though 1 has two claimants and 1 has none, and the second number is the one the capital dislikes.

Everything that leaves these 20 regions passes one of 6 gates, and not one of the 6 gates was chosen by the people who pay at it.

The tariff ledgers run deepest with the Crown, and coin buys the next gate. And the loop closes on itself: the Crown and the Temple are simply the two nearest to acting on it.

A town is two peoples with one name, and 4.2 in every hundred of them hold 47.6 coins in every hundred, though the sharpest of them is Rakemergate at 56 coins in the hundred.

The wire still does not reach 3 settlements, though a projection drew that line and 3 settlements fell the wrong side of it.

### What the Record Shows

Rakemergate closes the account with the figures. This world closed the gap, from 0.42 at the founding down to 0.20, and so the founding roll set 0.42 and the centuries did the rest. The rising of 1100 carried, and the ledger turned with it. The epochs group into 3 ages, the Age of the Gates (1000–1050, gini 0.33 → 0.31), the Age of Tariffs (1075–1125, gini 0.35 → 0.35), the Hollow Years (1150–1250, gini 0.33 → 0.20), though the same 10 epochs, grouped by where the curve bent. Counted over inhabited towns only, the bottom fifth carries 1.0× the top fifth's blight. And over 20 regions the poison fell on rich and poor alike. The adjacency graph gives Moran's I 0.091 on wealth, 0.151 on blight; some of that is guaranteed by construction, since the terrain is laid down with smoothing kernels and neighbours resemble each other before any economy runs (p 0.125 / 0.075, 199 permutations). The Kruth costs the country behind it 41% of the median settlement's earnings, distance held equal: the Kruth is the only term left once distance is controlled for. 27 points of the blight at Mirchfor were put in the water by towns above it, though the land set Mirchfor last in that order before anyone built anything. 8 regions hand a tariff to gate-holders they had no part in choosing, and the upkeep has lapsed on 1 of 5 crossings, which raises the charge again. The Kruth takes the rain: 42 on the windward side, 8 in the lee: the wind over these 20 regions was set before the first settlement and has not been asked since. Town sizes lie on a rank-size line: tail slope α 1.09, α 1.71 across the whole system: the law was written in at the founding by proportional growth, so it is not evidence of anything; the slope is, at α 1.09 and fit 0.95. Measured in distance saved, the skyway gives the shadow 36.8% and the open country 0%, and so boarding is a privilege of the owners' row, so the 36.8% goes to the country that can least afford the fare.

Read it as arithmetic rather than as argument: 28 figures over 20 regions, all of them recomputable.
